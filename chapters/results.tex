\section{Experiments and Results}

All four heuristics were evaluated on 294 held-out levels from the Boxoban medium set
under A* search with a maximum node expansion budget of 100,000. The CNN baseline
was additionally evaluated across a range of training set sizes to characterize its
data scaling behavior. Two reference points bracket the comparison: the
Manhattan-distance heuristic anchors the \emph{minimum} baseline that any learned
heuristic must beat to justify its training cost, and the CNN cost-to-go network
adapted from DeepCubeA anchors the \emph{upper-limit} baseline of what a
fully gradient-trained, parameter-rich architecture can achieve on the same inputs.
Table~\ref{tab:results_summary} summarizes the four solve rates side by side.

\subsection{Manhattan Distance (Minimum Baseline)}

The Manhattan-distance heuristic solves 87.4\% of the held-out levels (257/294) under
the same 100{,}000 expansion budget as every other method, despite having zero
trainable parameters and no knowledge of walls, deadlocks, or player position
(Figure~\ref{fig:manhattan_search}). Because the heuristic is provably admissible
under unit-cost pushes, every recovered solution is also optimal-length: the
average solution-length to optimal-length ratio is 1.00 across all solved levels.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{manhattan/best_search_results}
    \caption{Manhattan-distance heuristic A* search results on the 294 held-out medium-split levels under the 100{,}000 expansion budget. Solve rate: 87.4\% (257/294).}
    \label{fig:manhattan_search}
\end{figure}

That a parameter-free heuristic clears 87\% of the medium split establishes the
floor any learned heuristic must beat. Reframing the comparison around this floor
yields the central result of the thesis: pure NEAT, despite searching over both
topology and weights, fails to clear it, while CoDeepNEAT, which adds gradient-based
weight optimization to the evolutionary search, beats it.

\subsection{CNN Baseline (Upper-Limit Baseline)}

The CNN baseline is trained on 14{,}000 BFS-solved puzzles drawn from the Boxoban
medium split and evaluated on a disjoint set of 294 puzzles from the same split. With
this training set, the CNN achieves a 100\% solve rate (294/294) under the 100{,}000
node expansion budget, which serves as the upper-limit reference for the
neuroevolved heuristics. The 97.3\% figure that appears in the data-scaling analysis is
not a separate baseline but a single point from an earlier ablation that varied the
training set size: at the 14{,}000-level training point in that ablation the CNN solved
286/294, while subsequent runs with refined training (matching the configuration used
elsewhere in this thesis) reach 100\%. Both numbers are measured at the same 100{,}000
expansion budget on the same held-out 294 puzzles.

The data-scaling curve (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_scaling_solve}) shows that solve rate
rises steeply with training set size and then plateaus: 69.7\% at 100 levels,
94.9\% at 1{,}000 levels, 97.6\% at 5{,}000 levels, and saturates beyond
that. This plateau is the empirical justification for using a 14{,}000-level training
set rather than the full nine-million-level Boxoban corpus: the heuristic is not
data-limited beyond a few thousand training examples, and additional levels yield
diminishing returns at substantial BFS-solving cost.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{cnn/data_scaling_solve_rate}
    \caption{CNN baseline solve rate as a function of training set size.}
    \label{fig:cnn_scaling_solve}
\end{figure}

The 100{,}000 expansion budget is imposed deliberately so that the CNN, NEAT, and
CoDeepNEAT heuristics are
compared under identical search-time budgets. A less accurate heuristic will explore
more nodes per level to reach a solution, and without a fixed cap the comparison
would conflate heuristic quality with total search cost. The 100{,}000 budget was
chosen as a baseline to provide enough search-time budget for the baseline CNN heuristics; only 2.7\% of the puzzles were left unsolved within the budget. Under the same budget, other neuroevolution-based heuristics are meaningfully constrained. Reporting all three methods under the same cap therefore
isolates how heuristic accuracy translates into search efficiency.

\subsection{NEAT Discussion}

The NEAT heuristic solves 84.0\% of the 294 held-out levels (247/294, Figure~\ref{fig:neat_baseline_search}; full hyperparameter sweep in Appendix~\ref{app:neat_sweeps}), leaving 47 unsolved. This is 3.4 percentage points \emph{below} the parameter-free Manhattan-distance baseline (87.4\%, Figure~\ref{fig:manhattan_search}), so on this benchmark pure NEAT fails to clear the minimum baseline that any learned heuristic must beat to justify its training cost.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{neat_sweep/baseline}
    \caption{NEAT baseline (\texttt{conn\_del}=0.5) A* search results on the 294 held-out medium-split levels under the 100{,}000 expansion budget. Solve rate: 84.0\% (247/294).}
    \label{fig:neat_baseline_search}
\end{figure} The winner genome contains 4 internal
nodes and 21 enabled connections for a total of 25 trainable parameters. This is approximately 0.001\% of the 2{,}031{,}809-parameter CNN baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:neat_winner_topology} visualises the genome by mapping every enabled input-to-network connection back to its position in the $5 \times 10 \times 10$ input tensor: 12 of the 21 enabled connections feed directly into the output node, while the remaining 9 are split evenly across three hidden nodes (two input edges plus one hidden-to-output edge each). The connectivity is heavily biased toward the BOX channel, with secondary contributions from WALL, GOAL, and PLAYER, and only sparse use of the BG channel.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{neat_sweep/winner_topology}
    \caption{NEAT winner genome topology. Each scatter point on the five $10 \times 10$ channel grids marks a single enabled connection from an input cell into the network; marker shape indicates the target node ($\bigcirc$ output, $\square / \diamond / \triangle$ hidden nodes H1/H2/H3) and color encodes connection weight (red positive, blue negative). The lower panel shows the four-node internal topology with each hidden node's three-input fan-in summarised and the three hidden-to-output weights shown explicitly.}
    \label{fig:neat_winner_topology}
\end{figure}

The topology in Figure~\ref{fig:neat_winner_topology} is the most direct evidence
for why pure NEAT fails to clear the Manhattan floor. Out of $5 \times 10 \times 10
= 500$ candidate input cells, only 21 enabled connections survive, and the
connectivity is heavily concentrated on the BOX channel. Concentrating fan-in on
BOX is, on its own, a meaningful inductive bias: the cost-to-go of a Sokoban state
is dominated by which boxes are placed where, so a heuristic that reads box
positions strongly is at least pointing at the right input. The decisive failure
is what is missing alongside it. The Manhattan heuristic is accurate
because it pairs each box position with the nearest goal position; the evolved
genome reads box positions but draws only sparse, idiosyncratic taps from the GOAL
channel and almost nothing from PLAYER, so it has no way to compute even a rough
analogue of per-box distance-to-goal. Without GOAL coupling, BOX-channel weights
collapse into a position-specific look-up: certain board regions get rewarded as
``probably high cost'' and others as ``probably low cost'' regardless of where the
goals actually are on that puzzle.

That collapse is exactly what the negative-MAE fitness function rewards on the
training distribution. Long-solution puzzles, those with high ground-truth
cost-to-go, contribute disproportionately to absolute error: a single 70-step puzzle
mispredicted by 20 hurts the fitness more than ten 25-step puzzles each
mispredicted by 2. The survival pressure on the genome is therefore biased toward
fitting the heaviest-cost puzzles in the training set, and the cheapest way to
fit them with a 25-parameter network is to memorise spatial cues from box
positions that co-occur with long solutions. The resulting heuristic is not a
generalisable cost-to-go estimator; it is a small position-keyed regression onto
the high-cost tail of the training distribution. On unseen puzzles where the box
positions do not match those memorised patterns, the heuristic provides no
information beyond what the BOX-only inputs accidentally imply, and A* search
falls back to expanding broadly. This explains both why solve rate falls below
Manhattan (the analytical heuristic uses the GOAL channel that NEAT effectively
ignored) and why the failures are concentrated on puzzles whose box-and-goal
geometry the genome has no way to read jointly.

The deeper cause is granularity of the optimiser, not size of the genome. NEAT
adjusts weights only through Gaussian perturbation under the
\texttt{weight\_mutate\_power} schedule, and the hyperparameter sweep in
Appendix~\ref{app:neat_sweeps} confirms that varying this perturbation magnitude
between 0.3 (\texttt{wt\_fine}) and 0.8 (\texttt{wt\_coarse}) only moves solve rate
by a fraction of a percentage point and never crosses the Manhattan baseline. The
weight signal that a heuristic on $500$-dimensional binary inputs needs is finer
than what coordinate-wise random perturbation, even at small step sizes, can
reliably produce: a single useful weight update is too high-dimensional an event to
be reached by mutation, and the speciation that protects topology innovations also
protects weight configurations that have not yet been refined. Pure evolutionary
search is therefore the wrong tool for the weight-tuning half of the problem on
this input dimensionality.

The CoDeepNEAT result is the empirical confirmation of this diagnosis. CoDeepNEAT
keeps NEAT's evolutionary architecture search but replaces weight perturbation with
Adam gradient descent on each assembled genome, and that single substitution moves
solve rate from 83.9\% (below Manhattan) to 88.8\% (above Manhattan). The
architecture search itself does not change; what changes is that weights are now
optimised by a method that uses the actual gradient of the MAE loss instead of
isotropic Gaussian noise. The 4.9 percentage-point jump from pure NEAT to
CoDeepNEAT, and the simultaneous crossing of the Manhattan baseline, is therefore
attributable to gradient information rather than topology innovation, which is
exactly what the topology figure suggests should be limiting pure NEAT in the first
place.

\subsection{CoDeepNEAT Discussion}
CoDeepNEAT produced a higher solve rate of 88.8\% (261/294) compared to the 84.0\% of pure NEAT, and crucially also clears the 87.4\% Manhattan-distance baseline by 1.4 percentage points. CoDeepNEAT is therefore the only neuroevolved method in this thesis that beats the parameter-free analytical baseline; the gradient-descent phase that distinguishes it from pure NEAT is what closes the gap. The evolved best architecture is a $500 \to 320 \to 320 \to 1$ feed-forward network containing 263{,}361 trainable parameters which is about $7.7\times$ smaller than the CNN baseline but roughly $10{,}500\times$ larger than the NEAT genome. The improvement over pure NEAT is likely driven by the finetuned weights produced by the Adam optimizer during each genome's gradient-descent phase, which captures finer gradient information about the cost-to-go space than NEAT's pure weight perturbation can. However, this comes at the cost of three orders of magnitude more parameters in exchange for only a 4.8 percentage-point increase in solve rate over NEAT and a 1.4 percentage-point increase over the parameter-free Manhattan baseline.

\subsection{Interpretation of Findings}

The four-way comparison places the two neuroevolved heuristics between a clear lower
and upper reference. Manhattan distance, with zero parameters and no learning,
solves 87.4\% of the held-out levels, so the bar for a learned heuristic to be worth
training at all is roughly 87\%. The CNN cost-to-go network, with 2{,}031{,}809
parameters and full gradient training, solves 100\% of the same levels and
establishes the upper limit. The neuroevolved heuristics fall on opposite sides of
the Manhattan floor: pure NEAT, at 84.0\%, fails to clear the floor despite searching
over both topology and weights, while CoDeepNEAT, at 88.8\%, clears it.

The split between NEAT and CoDeepNEAT is the central empirical finding. Pure NEAT's
weight-perturbation search is overmatched by the dimensionality of the $500$-binary
input grid: 25 evolved parameters cannot extract a heuristic signal more accurate
than the per-box minimum-Manhattan-distance summation that the Manhattan baseline
already provides analytically. CoDeepNEAT swaps weight perturbation for full Adam
gradient descent on each assembled genome and recovers enough fine-grained gradient
information to beat the analytical baseline at $7.7\times$ fewer parameters than the
CNN. In other words, neuroevolution \emph{can} beat the Manhattan-distance baseline
on Boxoban, but only when the architectural search is paired with gradient-based
weight optimization; pure topology-and-weight evolution from scratch is not enough
on inputs of this dimensionality. The gap between CoDeepNEAT and the CNN
(11.2 percentage points) remains non-trivial, but the fact that a $7.7\times$
parameter reduction loses only $\sim$11\% solve rate \emph{and} clears the Manhattan
floor suggests that heuristic compression through neuroevolution is a direction
warranting further study.
