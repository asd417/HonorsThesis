\section{Experiments and Results}

All four heuristics were evaluated on 294 held-out levels from the Boxoban medium set
under A* search with a maximum node expansion budget of 100,000. The CNN baseline
was additionally evaluated across a range of training set sizes to characterize its
data scaling behavior. Two reference points bracket the comparison: the
Manhattan-distance heuristic anchors the \emph{minimum} baseline that any learned
heuristic must beat to justify its training cost, and the CNN cost-to-go network
adapted from DeepCubeA anchors the \emph{upper-limit} baseline of what a
fully gradient-trained, parameter-rich architecture can achieve on the same inputs.
Table~\ref{tab:results_summary} summarizes the four solve rates side by side.

\subsection{Manhattan Distance (Minimum Baseline)}

The Manhattan-distance heuristic solves 87.4\% of the held-out levels (257/294) under
the same 100{,}000 expansion budget as every other method, despite having zero
trainable parameters and computing only the per-box L1 distance to the nearest goal
cell (Figure~\ref{fig:manhattan_search}). The A* search itself applies dead-square
precomputation, frozen $2\times2$ deadlock detection, reverse-move suppression, and
state-hash deduplication identically to every heuristic in this thesis, so the
87.4\% figure isolates the contribution of the heuristic signal under search-side
pruning that is held fixed across all four methods. Because the heuristic is
provably admissible under unit-cost pushes, every recovered solution is also
optimal-length: the average solution-length to optimal-length ratio is 1.00 across
all solved levels.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{manhattan/best_search_results}
    \caption{Manhattan-distance heuristic A* search results on the 294 held-out medium-split levels under the 100{,}000 expansion budget. Solve rate: 87.4\% (257/294).}
    \label{fig:manhattan_search}
\end{figure}

A parameter-free heuristic clearing 87\% of the medium split establishes the floor
that any learned heuristic must beat to justify its training cost. Framing the
comparison around this floor yields the central result of the thesis: pure NEAT,
despite searching over both topology and weights, fails to clear it, while
CoDeepNEAT, which adds gradient-based weight optimization to the evolutionary
search, beats it.

\subsection{CNN Baseline (Upper-Limit Baseline)}

The CNN baseline is trained on the same 14{,}000 BFS-solved puzzles drawn from the Boxoban
medium split and evaluated on a disjoint set of 294 puzzles from the same split. With
this training set, the CNN achieves a 100\% solve rate (294/294) under the 100{,}000
node expansion budget, which serves as the upper-limit reference for the
neuroevolved heuristics. The data-scaling analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_scaling_solve})
is not a separate baseline but an earlier ablation that swept the training set size
under a less refined training configuration; its 14{,}000-trajectory point reached
98.6\% (290/294) rather than 100\%. Subsequent runs with the refined training configuration
used elsewhere in this thesis reach the 100\% reported above. Both numbers are measured
at the same 100{,}000 expansion budget on the same held-out 294 puzzles, so the gap
between them reflects training-side hyperparameter choices, not data quantity or evaluation protocol.

The data-scaling curve (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_scaling_solve}) shows that solve rate
rises steeply with training set size and then converges: 69.7\% at 100 trajectories,
94.6\% at 1{,}000 trajectories, 97.3\% at 5{,}000 trajectories, and 98.6\% at
14{,}000 trajectories under the ablation's training configuration. The marginal gain
from each tenfold increase in training data shrinks rapidly, and the later ablation
that retrained at 14{,}000 trajectories with refined hyperparameters reached the
100\% reported above, cementing 14{,}000 as the effective benchmark training set.
Together these results justify using 14{,}000 trajectories rather than the full
nine-million-level Boxoban corpus: the heuristic is not data-limited beyond a few
thousand training examples, and additional trajectories yield diminishing returns
at substantial BFS-solving cost.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{cnn/data_scaling_solve_rate}
    \caption{CNN baseline solve rate as a function of training set size.}
    \label{fig:cnn_scaling_solve}
\end{figure}

The 100{,}000 expansion budget is imposed deliberately so that the CNN, NEAT, and
CoDeepNEAT heuristics are compared under identical search-time budgets. A less
accurate heuristic will explore more nodes per level to reach a solution, and
without a fixed cap the comparison would conflate heuristic quality with total
search cost. The cap was chosen so that the CNN baseline could solve essentially
every level (it reaches 100\% at this budget), while still meaningfully
constraining the neuroevolved heuristics. Reporting all three methods under the
same cap therefore isolates how heuristic accuracy translates into search
efficiency.

\subsection{NEAT Discussion}

The NEAT heuristic solves 84.0\% of the 294 held-out levels (247/294, Figure~\ref{fig:neat_baseline_search}; full hyperparameter sweep in Appendix~\ref{app:neat_sweeps}), leaving 47 unsolved. This is 3.4 percentage points \emph{below} the parameter-free Manhattan-distance baseline (87.4\%, Figure~\ref{fig:manhattan_search}), so on this benchmark pure NEAT fails to clear the minimum baseline that any learned heuristic must beat to justify its training cost.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{neat_sweep/baseline}
    \caption{NEAT baseline (\texttt{conn\_del}=0.5) A* search results on the 294 held-out medium-split levels under the 100{,}000 expansion budget. Solve rate: 84.0\% (247/294).}
    \label{fig:neat_baseline_search}
\end{figure}

The winner genome contains 4 internal
nodes and 21 enabled connections for a total of 25 trainable parameters. This is approximately 0.001\% of the 2{,}031{,}809-parameter CNN baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:neat_winner_topology} visualizes the genome by mapping every enabled input-to-network connection back to its position in the $5 \times 10 \times 10$ input tensor: 12 of the 21 enabled connections feed directly into the output node, while the remaining 9 are split evenly across three hidden nodes (two input edges plus one hidden-to-output edge each). The connectivity is heavily biased toward the BOX channel, with secondary contributions from WALL, GOAL, and PLAYER, and only sparse use of the BG channel.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{neat_sweep/winner_topology}
    \caption{NEAT winner genome topology. Each scatter point on the five $10 \times 10$ channel grids marks a single enabled connection from an input cell into the network; marker shape indicates the target node ($\bigcirc$ output, $\square / \diamond / \triangle$ hidden nodes H1/H2/H3) and color encodes connection weight (red positive, blue negative). The lower panel shows the four-node internal topology with each hidden node's three-input fan-in summarized and the three hidden-to-output weights shown explicitly.}
    \label{fig:neat_winner_topology}
\end{figure}

The topology in Figure~\ref{fig:neat_winner_topology} is the most direct evidence
for why pure NEAT fails to clear the Manhattan floor. Out of $5 \times 10 \times 10
= 500$ candidate input cells, only 21 enabled connections survive, and the
connectivity is heavily concentrated on the BOX channel. Concentrating fan-in on
BOX is, on its own, a meaningful inductive bias: the cost-to-go of a Sokoban state
is dominated by which boxes are placed where, so a heuristic that reads box
positions strongly is at least pointing at the right input. The decisive failure
is what is missing alongside it. The Manhattan heuristic is accurate
because it pairs each box position with the nearest goal position; the evolved
genome reads box positions but draws only sparse, idiosyncratic taps from the GOAL
channel and almost nothing from PLAYER, so it has no way to compute even a rough
analogue of per-box distance-to-goal. Without GOAL coupling, BOX-channel weights
collapse into a position-specific look-up: certain board regions get rewarded as
``probably high cost'' and others as ``probably low cost'' regardless of where the
goals actually are on that puzzle.

That collapse is exactly what the negative-MAE fitness function rewards on the
training distribution. Long-solution puzzles, those with high ground-truth
cost-to-go, contribute disproportionately to absolute error: a single 70-step puzzle
mispredicted by 20 hurts the fitness more than ten 25-step puzzles each
mispredicted by 2. The survival pressure on the genome is therefore biased toward
fitting the heaviest-cost puzzles in the training set, and the cheapest way to
fit them with a 25-parameter network is to memorize spatial cues from box
positions that co-occur with long solutions. The resulting heuristic is not a
generalizable cost-to-go estimator; it is a small position-keyed regression onto
the high-cost tail of the training distribution. On unseen puzzles where the box
positions do not match those memorized patterns, the heuristic provides no
information beyond what the BOX-only inputs accidentally imply, and A* search
falls back to expanding broadly. This explains both why solve rate falls below
Manhattan (the analytical heuristic uses the GOAL channel that NEAT effectively
ignored) and why solve rates are nearly uniform across the NEAT hyperparameter
sweep: when the heuristic carries little information beyond a position-keyed
prior, the search is led primarily by the shared search-side pruning machinery
(dead-square precomputation, frozen $2\times2$ deadlock detection, reverse-move
suppression, and state-hash deduplication) rather than by the heuristic itself,
so swapping NEAT hyperparameters moves solve rate by only a fraction of a
percentage point.

The deeper cause is granularity of the optimizer. NEAT
adjusts weights only through Gaussian perturbation under the
\texttt{weight\_mutate\_power} schedule, and the hyperparameter sweep in
Appendix~\ref{app:neat_sweeps} confirms that varying this perturbation magnitude
between 0.3 (\texttt{wt\_fine}) and 0.8 (\texttt{wt\_coarse}) only moves solve rate
by a fraction of a percentage point and never crosses the Manhattan baseline. The
weight signal that a heuristic on $500$-dimensional binary inputs needs is finer
than what coordinate-wise random perturbation, even at small step sizes, can
reliably produce: a single useful weight update is too high-dimensional an event to
be reached by mutation, and the speciation that protects topology innovations also
protects weight configurations that have not yet been refined. Pure evolutionary
search is therefore the wrong tool for the weight-tuning half of the problem on
this input dimensionality.

The CoDeepNEAT result is the empirical confirmation of this diagnosis. CoDeepNEAT
keeps NEAT's evolutionary architecture search but replaces weight perturbation with
Adam gradient descent on each assembled genome, and that single substitution moves
solve rate from 83.9\% (below Manhattan) to 88.8\% (above Manhattan). The substitution replaces isotropic Gaussian weight perturbation with Adam steps
driven by the actual MAE gradient, which is a coordinate-aligned signal rather
than a random one. The 4.9 percentage-point jump from pure NEAT to
CoDeepNEAT, and the simultaneous crossing of the Manhattan baseline, is therefore
attributable to gradient information rather than topology innovation. This is
consistent with the topology analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:neat_winner_topology},
which identifies weight-perturbation granularity, rather than topological
expressiveness, as the binding constraint on pure NEAT.

\subsection{CoDeepNEAT Discussion}
CoDeepNEAT produces a higher solve rate of 88.8\% (261/294) than pure NEAT's 84.0\%, and crucially also clears the Manhattan-distance baseline by 1.4 percentage points. CoDeepNEAT is therefore the only neuroevolved method in this thesis that beats the parameter-free analytical baseline; the gradient-descent phase that distinguishes it from pure NEAT is what closes the gap. The evolved best architecture is a $500 \to 320 \to 320 \to 1$ feed-forward network containing 263{,}361 trainable parameters, about $7.7\times$ smaller than the CNN baseline but roughly $10{,}500\times$ larger than the NEAT genome. The improvement over pure NEAT is likely driven by the fine-tuned weights produced by the Adam optimizer during each genome's gradient-descent phase, which captures finer gradient information about the cost-to-go space than NEAT's pure weight perturbation can. However, this improvement requires roughly $10{,}500\times$ more parameters than the NEAT genome, yielding only a 4.8 percentage-point increase in solve rate over NEAT and a 1.4 percentage-point increase over the parameter-free Manhattan baseline.

\subsection{Interpretation of Findings}

The split between NEAT and CoDeepNEAT, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results_summary}, is the central empirical finding. Pure NEAT's weight-perturbation search is overmatched by the dimensionality of the $500$-binary input grid: a 25-parameter genome cannot extract a heuristic signal more accurate than the per-box minimum-Manhattan-distance summation that the Manhattan baseline already provides analytically. CoDeepNEAT, by contrast, clears that baseline. The contrast establishes the central claim of the thesis: neuroevolution \emph{can} beat the Manhattan-distance baseline on Boxoban, but only when architectural search is paired with gradient-based weight optimization; pure topology-and-weight evolution from scratch is not enough on inputs of this dimensionality. The remaining gap to the CNN is non-trivial, but the fact that a $7.7\times$ parameter reduction still clears the Manhattan floor suggests that heuristic compression through neuroevolution is a direction warranting further study.
