\section{Experiments and Results}

All three heuristics were evaluated on 294 held-out levels from the Boxoban medium set
under A* search with a maximum node expansion budget of 100,000. The CNN baseline
was additionally evaluated across a range of training set sizes to characterize its
data scaling behavior.

\subsection{CNN Baseline}

The CNN baseline is trained on 14{,}000 BFS-solved puzzles drawn from the Boxoban
medium split and evaluated on a disjoint set of 294 puzzles from the same split. With
this training set, the CNN achieves a 100\% solve rate (294/294) under the 100{,}000
node expansion budget. The 97.3\% figure that appears in the data-scaling analysis is
not a separate baseline but a single point from an earlier ablation that varied the
training set size: at the 14{,}000-level training point in that ablation the CNN solved
286/294, while subsequent runs with refined training (matching the configuration used
elsewhere in this thesis) reach 100\%. Both numbers are measured at the same 100{,}000
expansion budget on the same held-out 294 puzzles.

The data-scaling curve (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_scaling_solve}) shows that solve rate
rises steeply with training set size and then plateaus: 69.7\% at 100 levels,
94.9\% at 1{,}000 levels, 97.6\% at 5{,}000 levels, and saturates beyond
that. This plateau is the empirical justification for using a 14{,}000-level training
set rather than the full nine-million-level Boxoban corpus: the heuristic is not
data-limited beyond a few thousand training examples, and additional levels yield
diminishing returns at substantial BFS-solving cost.

The 100{,}000 expansion budget is imposed deliberately so that the CNN, NEAT, and
CoDeepNEAT heuristics are
compared under identical search-time budgets. A less accurate heuristic will explore
more nodes per level to reach a solution, and without a fixed cap the comparison
would conflate heuristic quality with total search cost. The 100{,}000 budget was
chosen to be generous enough that the CNN rarely hits it --- the 2.7\% of unsolved
levels reflect the tail of the expansion distribution, not a failure of the learned
heuristic --- while being tight enough that the smaller neuroevolved heuristics are
meaningfully constrained. Reporting all three methods under the same cap therefore
isolates how heuristic accuracy translates into search efficiency.

\subsection{NEAT Discussion}

The NEAT heuristic solves 84.0\% of the 294 held-out levels (247/294, see Appendix
\ref{app:neat_sweeps}), leaving 47 unsolved. The winner genome contains 4 internal
nodes and 208 enabled connections for a total of 212 trainable parameters --- roughly
0.01\% of the 2{,}031{,}809-parameter CNN baseline. Because every run is capped at
the same 100{,}000-expansion budget used for the CNN, failure is overwhelmingly
attributable to heuristic inaccuracy rather than compute starvation: the 212-parameter
genome produces coarse cost-to-go estimates that mislead the search priority, causing
A* to expand large regions of the state space around misleading local minima before
either finding a solution or exhausting the budget. The network simply lacks the
capacity to distinguish structurally similar states whose true cost-to-go differs
sharply, so the search cannot rank successors reliably near deadlock-adjacent
configurations. Raising the expansion budget would recover some of these levels, but
the comparison in this thesis deliberately fixes the budget so that heuristic quality,
not search effort, is what varies across methods. For the NEAT network to produce a
better heuristic function, it would need to grow into a larger topology with more
trainable parameters by modifying the evolution configuration --- for example, the
node-addition probability could be increased to favor adding hidden nodes throughout
the generations.

\subsection{CoDeepNEAT Discussion}
CoDeepNEAT produced a higher solve rate of 88.8\% (261/294) compared to the 84.0\% of pure NEAT. The evolved best architecture is a $500 \to 320 \to 320 \to 1$ feed-forward network containing 263{,}361 trainable parameters --- about $7.7\times$ smaller than the CNN baseline but roughly $1{,}240\times$ larger than the NEAT genome. The improvement over pure NEAT is likely driven by the finetuned weights produced by the Adam optimizer during each genome's gradient-descent phase, which captures finer gradient information about the cost-to-go space than NEAT's pure weight perturbation can. However, this comes at the cost of three orders of magnitude more parameters in exchange for only a 4.8 percentage-point increase in solve rate over NEAT.

\subsection{Interpretation of Findings}

The results confirm that neuroevolution can produce heuristics that are radically more
parameter-efficient while remaining useful for A* search. NEAT converges to a genome
with 4 internal nodes and 208 enabled connections --- 212 trainable parameters in
total, approximately 0.01\% of the CNN baseline's 2{,}031{,}809 parameters. CoDeepNEAT
discovers a $500 \to 320 \to 320 \to 1$ architecture with 263{,}361 trainable
parameters, roughly $7.7\times$ smaller than the CNN baseline. The gap in solve rate
relative to the CNN (84.0\% and 88.8\% versus 100\%) is non-trivial, but the degree
to which both neuroevolved heuristics close this gap while operating at a fraction of
the parameter budget suggests that heuristic compression through neuroevolution is a
direction warranting further study.
