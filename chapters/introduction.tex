\section{Background and Motivation}

Sokoban is a combinatorial planning puzzle invented by Hiroyuki Imabayashi in Japan in 1981.
A player occupies a grid of cells containing walls, boxes, and goal positions, and must push
every box onto a goal by moving up, down, left, or right. The rules are simple, but its puzzles
can be extremely complex. Sokoban has been shown to be both NP-hard \cite{fryers1995sokoban}
and PSPACE-complete \cite{culberson1997sokoban}, placing it at the upper limits of complexity
for discrete planning problems.

The difficulty arises from two main properties. First, the game is irreversible. Once a box is
pushed against a wall or into a corner that is not a goal position, it can never be retrieved
since the player cannot pull the box. This is a deadlock that forces the player to fully reset
(or undo, depending on the version of the game).
The existence of deadlocks constrains the agent from exploring the space stochastically, as a
single incorrect move can invalidate all previous moves. This poses a significant challenge for
exploration-based agents, which are the current standard of reinforcement learning. Second, rewards
are sparse. For a box to be pushed to the correct position, the agent must first perform a long
list of actions that may or may not have a clear reward. An agent can always push the box toward a goal,
but this does not guarantee that the box can be pushed onto the goal from that specific direction.
The lack of accurate feedback means there is no gradient signal for simple training-based agents.
Together, these properties make Sokoban a canonical stress-test for planning agents.

\subsection*{A Brief History of Sokoban Solvers}

Early approaches to automated Sokoban solving, developed through the 1990s and early
2000s, were largely rule-based. Solvers such as YASS \cite{damgaard_yass} and Sokoban++ 
\cite{wits_sokobanpp} relied on handcrafted heuristics, deadlock tables, and macro-moves. 
These a priori heuristics were designed by human experts to capture common patterns of solutions 
in Sokoban puzzles by avoiding deadlocks. These systems achieved impressive results on small
curated puzzle sets such as XSokoban \cite{junghanns1999domain}, but they required significant
domain knowledge to implement.

The 2010s brought reinforcement learning methods to Sokoban. Deep Q-Networks and policy
gradient agents \cite{groshev2018learning} were applied to the puzzle, with some
imagination-augmented architectures \cite{NEURIPS2017_7ca66fc0} using a learned world model to 
simulate future states before committing to an action. These model-based agents demonstrated 
that a neural network could implicitly learn to plan ahead without handcrafted rules, but they 
remained sample-inefficient and struggled on the most complex instances.

A more powerful paradigm emerged from work on the Rubik's Cube. Agostinelli et al.\
\cite{agostinelli2019solving} showed that by training a deep neural network on a reverse trajectory
that expanded the state space from the solved state, the network could learn a generalized cost-to-go
function that could serve as the heuristic function in an A* search, enabling an
agent to solve the Rubik's Cube optimally without any domain-specific knowledge. The reverse trajectory
is generated by scrambling the Rubik's Cube from the solved state with random moves and labeled by the
number of moves applied. Subsequent work extended this framework to Sokoban and demonstrated that the
same cost-to-go approach, guided by A* search, can solve highly complex puzzle instances including
Sokoban-like puzzles \cite{agostinelli2021admissible}.

\subsection*{The Boxoban Benchmark}

Despite these advances, no study has applied the cost-to-go A* framework to Boxoban, a
large-scale Sokoban dataset released by Google consisting of nine million procedurally
generated $10\times10$ levels. Boxoban was created to benchmark agents that must plan
over long horizons on a diverse distribution of puzzles, specifically to stress-test
imagination-based planning models: approaches that use a learned world model to internally
simulate sequences of future states before selecting an action \cite{NEURIPS2017_7ca66fc0}.
In such models, the agent unrolls the world model forward from the current state, generating 
imaginary trajectories that it encodes alongside real observations to inform its policy. Although Boxoban was originally framed as a stress test for imagination-based agents, it is a challenging testbed for reinforcement learning agents more broadly: the long planning horizons, irreversible moves, and sparse reward structure of Sokoban make it a valid benchmark for any method that must learn to plan, and the fixed $10 \times 10$ grid size yields a compact $5 \times 10 \times 10$ multi-hot state representation that any architecture can consume directly without resizing or per-level preprocessing. The scale of nine million unique levels prevents any solver from memorizing solutions and instead demands genuine generalization
across the full distribution of puzzle configurations.

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes four primary contributions, organized as a pair of reference
baselines bracketing two neuroevolved heuristics.

First, we establish a parameter-free Manhattan-distance heuristic, summing each
box's minimum Manhattan distance to a goal cell, as the \emph{minimum} baseline.
Because the heuristic is provably admissible under unit-cost pushes, it is the
classical analytical reference any learned heuristic must beat to justify the cost
of training.

Second, we implement a CNN baseline heuristic with four layers of convolution and
2 million trainable parameters, adapted from the DeepCubeA cost-to-go formulation,
as the \emph{upper-limit} baseline. The loss function was modified to punish
overestimation of value more than underestimation to encourage training an
admissible heuristic.

Third, we apply NEAT \cite{stanley2002evolving} to evolve a feed-forward heuristic
network from a minimal starting topology. NEAT searches simultaneously over network topology and weights,
adding nodes and connections only where doing so improves the fitness signal, and uses speciation to protect structural innovations
from premature elimination.

Fourth, we apply CoDeepNEAT \cite{miikkulainen2019evolving} to coevolve populations of
dense-dropout modules and blueprint topologies, with each assembled genome trained by
gradient descent for a fixed number of epochs per generation. This hybrid approach
combines the architectural flexibility of evolutionary search with the weight-optimization
efficiency of gradient descent, producing heuristics that are more lightweight than the CNN
baseline while maintaining competitive accuracy.

All learned heuristics are trained on cost-to-go labels derived from Breadth-First Search
solution trajectories over the Boxoban dataset and evaluated on unseen levels by mean
absolute error and A* solve rate, with the Manhattan and CNN baselines providing the
lower and upper reference points for the neuroevolved methods.
