\section{Background and Motivation}

Sokoban is a combinatorial planning puzzle invented by Hiroyuki Imabayashi in Japan in 1981.
A player occupies a grid of cells containing walls, boxes, and goal positions, and must push
every box onto a goal by moving up, down, left, or right. The rules are simple but its puzzles 
can be extremely complex. Sokoban has been shown to be both NP-hard \cite{fryers1995sokoban} 
and PSPACE-complete \cite{culberson1997sokoban}, placing it at the upper limits of complexity 
for discrete planning problems.

The difficulty arises from two main properties. First, the game is irreversible. Once a box is 
pushed against a wall or into a corner that is not a goal position, it can never be retrieved 
since the player can not pull the box. This is a deadlock which forces the player to fully reset 
(or undo depending on the version of the game). 
The existence of deadlock constrains the agent from exploring the space stochastically as a 
single incorrect move can invalidate all previous moves. This poses a significant challenge for 
exploration-based agents which is the current standard of reinforcement learning. Second, rewards 
are sparse. For a box to be pushed to the correct position, the agent must first perform long 
list of actions which may or may not have a clear reward. An agent can always push the box towards a goal
but this does not guarantee that the box can be pushed onto the goal from that specific direction. 
The lack of accurate feedback means there is no gradient signal for a simple training based agents. 
Together these properties make Sokoban a canonical stress-test for planning agents.

\subsection*{A Brief History of Sokoban Solvers}

Early approaches to automated Sokoban solving, developed through the 1990s and early
2000s, were largely rule-based. Solvers such as YASS \cite{damgaard_yass} and Sokoban++ \cite{wits_sokobanpp} 
relied on handcrafted heuristics, deadlock tables, and macro-moves


--- carefully enumerated patterns that allowed
the solver to detect unsolvable states or to move boxes across the board in a single logical
step. These systems achieved impressive results on the small curated puzzle sets of that
era, but they required significant domain knowledge to implement and did not generalise
beyond the configurations their designers had anticipated.

The 2010s brought reinforcement learning methods to Sokoban. Deep Q-Networks and policy
gradient agents \cite{NEURIPS2017_7ca66fc0} were applied to the puzzle, with some
imagination-augmented architectures using a learned world model to simulate future states
before committing to an action. These model-based agents demonstrated that a neural
network could implicitly learn to plan ahead without handcrafted rules, but they remained
sample-inefficient and struggled on the most complex instances.

A more powerful paradigm emerged from work on the Rubik's Cube. Agostinelli et al.\
\cite{agostinelli2019solving} showed that a deep neural network trained to predict the
cost-to-go --- the minimum number of moves remaining to a solution --- could serve as the
heuristic function in a Weighted A* search, enabling an agent to solve the Rubik's Cube
optimally without any domain-specific knowledge. The network is trained by backward
induction: starting from the solved state, random scrambles of increasing length produce
training states, each labelled with the number of scrambles applied as its ground-truth
cost-to-go. A subsequent work extended this framework to Sokoban and demonstrated that
the same cost-to-go approach, guided by A* search, can solve highly complex puzzle
instances \cite{agostinelli2021admissible}.

\subsection*{The Boxoban Benchmark}

Despite these advances, no study has applied the cost-to-go A* framework to Boxoban, a
large-scale Sokoban dataset released by Google consisting of nine million procedurally
generated $10\times10$ levels. Boxoban was created to benchmark agents that must plan
over long horizons on a diverse distribution of puzzles, specifically to stress-test
imagination-based planning models: approaches that use a learned world model to internally
simulate or ``imagine'' sequences of future states before selecting an action
\cite{NEURIPS2017_7ca66fc0}. In such models, the agent unrolls the world model forward
from the current state, generating imaginary trajectories that it encodes alongside
real observations to inform its policy. Because the world model is imperfect, the agent
must learn to plan despite model errors --- making Boxoban's combinatorial depth and
irreversibility an especially demanding test. The scale of nine million unique levels
prevents any solver from memorising solutions and instead demands genuine generalisation
across the full distribution of puzzle configurations.

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes three primary contributions. First, we implement a CNN baseline
heuristic --- a fixed four-layer convolutional network with 2,031,809 parameters trained
by gradient descent with an asymmetric Huber loss --- and evaluate its cost-to-go
prediction accuracy and Weighted A* solve rate on Boxoban as the reference point for
comparison.

Second, we apply NEAT \cite{stanley2002evolving} to evolve a feed-forward heuristic
network from a minimal starting topology of approximately 26 parameters. NEAT searches
simultaneously over network topology and weights, adding nodes and connections only where
doing so improves the fitness signal, and uses speciation to protect structural innovations
from premature elimination.

Third, we apply CoDeepNEAT \cite{miikkulainen2019evolving} to coevolve populations of
dense-dropout modules and blueprint topologies, with each assembled genome trained by
gradient descent for a fixed number of epochs per generation. This hybrid approach
combines the architectural flexibility of evolutionary search with the weight-optimisation
efficiency of gradient descent, producing heuristics that are between 12$\times$ and
63$\times$ smaller than the CNN baseline.

All three heuristics are trained on cost-to-go labels derived from Breadth-First Search
solution trajectories over the Boxoban dataset and evaluated on held-out levels by mean
absolute error and Weighted A* solve rate.
