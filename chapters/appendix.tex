\section{Manhattan Distance Baseline Results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{manhattan/best_search_results}
    \caption{Manhattan-distance heuristic A* search results on the 294 held-out medium-split levels under the 100{,}000 expansion budget. Solve rate: 87.4\% (257/294). Average solution-length to optimal-length ratio: 1.00 (the heuristic is admissible).}
    \label{fig:manhattan_search_appendix}
\end{figure}

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:manhattan_search_appendix} shows that every solved
level lies exactly on the $y = x$ optimality line: the A* solution length equals the
optimal solution length on all 257 solved puzzles, yielding an average solution-length
to optimal-length ratio of 1.00. This is not an empirical accident but a guaranteed
consequence of using an admissible heuristic with A* search. The Manhattan-distance
heuristic, summing the per-box minimum Manhattan distance to any goal cell, is provably
admissible under unit-cost pushes because pushing a box from $(r_1, c_1)$ to
$(r_2, c_2)$ requires at least $|r_1 - r_2| + |c_1 - c_2|$ push moves regardless of
walls, deadlocks, or interactions between boxes. A* search with an admissible
heuristic is itself guaranteed to return optimal-cost solutions whenever it terminates
successfully, so a 1.00 length ratio across every solved level is the expected
behavior of this configuration rather than an indication that the heuristic is
particularly accurate. Heuristic accuracy in this setting is reflected only in the
left panel, the search-cost (node-expansion) distribution and the resulting solve rate
under the 100{,}000-expansion budget; optimality of the recovered solutions is fixed
by the admissibility of the heuristic and is not a free parameter of the comparison.
